# Literature & Primary Sources Directory (`/literature`)

This directory contains all academic studies, official statistical reports, and register tables used across this project, complete with full bibliographic citations, DOIs, URLs, methodological classifications, and summaries of empirical findings.

Special emphasis is placed on the **joint distribution of Age $\times$ Socioeconomic Status (SES / poverty / precarity)** and its criminological interaction with delinquency trajectories over the life course (adolescent peak vs. persistent adult precarity).

### 3.1 Destatis Microcensus / EU-SILC (2024): Bivariate Age-Poverty Cross-Tabulation
* **Data Source**: Microcensus sub-sample Income and Living Conditions (EU-SILC 2023, Genesis Table 12211).
* **Objective**: Measure relative income poverty (net equivalised disposable income < 60% of national median) differentiated by age bracket and migration status in Germany.
* **Empirical Cross-Tabulation (At-Risk-of-Poverty Rates in %)**:
  * **Children and Adolescents (< 18 years)**: Germans without migration background: **14.8%** | Foreigners / Non-Germans: **36.2%** (Disparity: 2.45×)
  * **Young Adults (18–24 years, criminological high-risk window)**: Germans: **22.1%** | Foreigners: **48.6%** (Disparity: 2.20×)
  * **Middle-Aged Adults (25–49 years)**: Germans: **11.5%** | Foreigners: **31.8%** (Disparity: 2.77×)
  * **Older Working-Age Adults (50–64 years)**: Germans: **12.0%** | Foreigners: **28.4%** (Disparity: 2.37×)
  * **Seniors (65 years and older)**: Germans: **15.2%** | Foreigners: **26.7%** (Disparity: 1.76×)
* **Scientific Relevance for the Investigation**:
  * Young adults (18–24 years) exhibit the **highest poverty rate** in Germany across all demographic groups.
  * In Panel E, this demographic structure is integrated across the harmonized 14–30 window: the official youth population share ($P(Y \mid g)$: 15.9% German vs. 31.2% non-German, 1.96× overhang), the empirical male sex ratio across 14–30 ($P(M \mid Y, g)$: 51.2% vs. 55.0% from Census 2022 / AZR 12521-04), and the population-weighted poverty rate for young men ($16.32\%$ vs. $39.05\%$, integrating EU-SILC cohorts with $k = 0.968$) via the **conditional chain rule**:
    $$P(\text{Risk} \mid g) = P(Y \mid g) \cdot P(M \mid Y, g) \cdot P(\text{Pov} \mid Y, M, g)$$
    This yields an audited point estimate of **1.3%** (Germans: $0.159 \times 0.512 \times 0.1632$) vs. **6.7%** (non-Germans: $0.312 \times 0.550 \times 0.3905$), a **5.04×** disparity, accompanied by Fréchet–Hoeffding analytical bounds ([0.0%, 2.6%] vs. [0.0%, 12.2%]) and Plackett copula sensitivity ($\text{OR} \in [0.8, 1.5]$: 4.81× to 5.21×). Legacy heuristics assuming 86.5% precarity have been completely replaced by this closed official conditional chain.

### 3.2 Grabka & Goebel (DIW Berlin, 2020 / SOEP v38): Life Course and Poverty Dynamics
* **Data Source**: German Socio-Economic Panel (SOEP), waves 1984–2020, representative longitudinal household panel ($N > 30,000$ individuals annually).
* **Methodology**: Tracking individual equivalised incomes and transition probabilities across distinct life phases.
* **Core Findings & Metrics**:
  * The age-poverty distribution follows a pronounced **U-curve**: The transition from vocational training/university into the labor market (ages 18–25) is characterized by atypical employment, low individual labor earnings, and household formation.
  * The poverty risk in the 18–25 cohort stands at **24.8%**, more than double that of the stable prime-earning phase (ages 35–55 at **11.7%**, ratio: **2.12×**).
  * Immigrant households also possess significantly fewer wealth buffers, meaning unexpected income shocks directly translate into acute material hardship.

### 3.3 Hällsten, Szulkin & Sarnecki (2013, *Br. J. Criminol.*): Multivariate Matching of Immigrant Cohorts
* **Data Basis**: Complete Swedish register cohort of 65,042 youth growing up in the Stockholm metropolitan area, linked to tax records, social welfare rolls, and criminal conviction registers up to age 30.
* **Methodological Design**: Successive regression of registered offending on immigrant status controlling for:
  1. Age and biological sex (baseline cohort)
  2. Parental socioeconomic resources (parental income deciles, unemployment, welfare receipt)
  3. Neighborhood deprivation and segregation index
  4. Academic GPA / school performance
* **Key Findings**:
  * Among male youth, the crude disparity between natives and 1st/2nd generation immigrants shrinks by **50% to 75%** once parental SES and neighborhood deprivation are controlled.
  * Among female youth, the disparity completely disappears (**100% mediation/reduction**).
  * **Causal Conclusion**: Crime disparities are primarily the price of structural inequality and economic marginalization rather than cultural or ethnic traits.

### 3.4 Laub & Sampson (2003, Harvard Univ. Press): Life Course Research to Age 70
* **Sample**: 1,000 men from the Boston Glueck study, tracked across 50 years up to age 70 (combining official arrest histories and qualitative life history interviews).
* **Age $\times$ SES Interaction Mechanism**:
  * Delinquency is not predetermined across the entire lifespan by early childhood traits. Rather, **socioeconomic turning points in adulthood (starting at ages 24–25)** serve as the primary drivers of desistance.
  * Men with stable, well-paying full-time employment show a **39% reduction** in annual arrest hazard ($\text{OR} = 0.61$, $p < 0.01$).
  * Absent these SES resources (chronic unemployment, precarity, social isolation), offending rates remain elevated well into the 40s and 50s (*Persistent Cumulative Disadvantage*).

---

### 3.5 Loeber & Farrington (2012, Oxford Univ. Press): Delinquency Trajectories and Deprivation
* **Sample**: Cambridge Study in Delinquent Development (London, $N = 411$) and Pittsburgh Youth Study ($N = 1,517$).
* **Core Findings**:
  * Chronic poverty modulates both the **peak amplitude** (elevated incidence of serious violent offending at ages 17–20) and the **rate of desistance**.
  * Youth from stable middle-class families exhibit rapid desistance beginning at age 21 (half-life $\approx 4$ years), whereas youth from chronically disadvantaged backgrounds experience a desistance delay by a factor of **2.4×**.
  * Stouthamer-Loeber (2010) shows in PYS longitudinal data: 52% to 57% persist between ages 20–25, but between ages 25–30 persistence drops by two-thirds to 16%–19%.

### 3.6 Baier, Pfeiffer, Simonson & Rabold (2009, KFN FB 107 / BMI): Jugendliche in Deutschland als Opfer und Täter von Gewalt ($N = 44,610$)
* **Data Source**: Nationally representative student survey commissioned by the Federal Ministry of the Interior (BMI) covering 44,610 9th-graders across Germany.
* **Empirical Findings & Benchmarks**:
  1. **Dual Police-Reporting Bias by Ethnic Dyad (Victim/Offender)**:
     * *German victim, German offender*: Reporting rate **19.5%** (Chapter 4, p. 11).
     * *German victim, young immigrant offender*: Reporting rate **29.3%** (**1.50× higher reporting propensity**).
     * *German offender attacking immigrant victim*: Reporting rate only **18.9%**.
  2. **Multivariate Decomposition of Youth Violence (Figure 5.24, p. 86)**:
     * *Crude 12-month self-reported prevalence*: Germans **11.5%** vs. Turkish-origin **20.3%** (ratio 1.77×) vs. former Yugoslavia **22.1%** (ratio 1.92×).
     * *Under identical living conditions (SES, peer networks, school track, family background)*:
       * Germans ($N = 17,318$): **6.4%**
       * Turkish-origin ($N = 505$): **5.9%**
       * Former Yugoslavia / Albania ($N = 173$): **5.7%**
       * **Finding**: Under identical socioeconomic conditions, youth with a migration background exhibit **no higher rate of violent offending** ($p > 0.05$, n.s.), displaying slightly lower self-reported prevalence.
  3. **Educational Stratification**:
     * Basic-track students (*Hauptschule*) display a **19.2%** violent offender rate, **2.40× higher** than academic-track students (*Gymnasium*, **8.0%**, Table 5.2, p. 67).
  4. **Sex Disparity**:
     * Boys display a **20.3%** violent offending prevalence, **3.17× higher** than girls (**6.4%**, Table 5.2, p. 67).

**Causal Nuance & Sibling Fixed-Effects Attenuation (Sariaslan et al. 2014, 2021)**:
- The effect ratios ($Y_i \approx 2.34\text{--}3.10\times$) in this meta-regression represent **macro-observational population disparities**. Youth growing up in low-income or welfare-dependent households exhibit more than double the observed rate of violent crime convictions.
- However, rigorous sibling-comparison studies (notably Sariaslan et al. 2014 in Sweden and Sariaslan et al. 2021 in Finland) demonstrate that within-family discordant sibling models attenuate the hazard ratio for family income substantially (attenuating from $HR \approx 2.42\text{--}2.52$ at the population level to $HR \approx 1.10\text{--}1.12$, virtually null).
- This vital distinction demonstrates that pure monetary income is an **empirical index/proxy of clustered structural disadvantage** (school segregation, labor market barriers, housing instability, intergenerational capital deficits, discriminatory policing) rather than an isolated mechanical lever that can be adjusted in isolation.
- Accordingly, the OECD meta-regression models the **aggregate population risk gradient** associated with socioeconomic stratification, and must not be misinterpreted as an unconfounded guarantee that isolated income transfers alone would eliminate delinquency.
